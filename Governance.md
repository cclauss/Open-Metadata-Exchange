# Open Metadata Exchange

## Organization and Governance

The Open Metadata Exchange (OME) organization should be a collection of willing participants who seek to connect Open Education Resources libraries by using the OME Network to publish and subscribe to OER metadata in a shared, decentralized ecosystem. The organization should advocate the sharing of Open Educational Resources on a not-for-profit basis.  Substantial intellectual gains can be achieved by participating in metadata stewardship, as a public service to strengthen discovery, preservation, and collaboration across libraries and educational institutions. The Open Metadata Exchange is built around a distributed network model: a common metadata model, networked nodes, plugin-driven integrations, and a frontend user interface that helps librarians publish and subscribe to OER information on globally. This architecture supports the central goal of making OER discovery easier without requiring each library to reinvent its own cataloging and publishing workflows.

The governance model should be practical, transparent, and designed for sustainable growth. The OER Steering Committee should be formed in the Autumn of 2026 and should be composed of managers of OER libraries and platforms. In its first year of operations, the Steering Committee should have six members and meet (mostly virtually) monthly. This smaller initial structure will allow rapid action while the organization is still establishing its operating model, technical practices, and partner relationships. In the second year, the Steering Committee should add two additional members, bringing the total to eight, and continue to provide a workable governing body with enough diversity of perspective to inform strategic decisions. In the third year, it should add two more members for a total of ten and meet every two months. This phased approach helps the project scale from a focused pilot effort to a broader network of participating institutions without losing momentum or clarity of direction.

The Steering Committee should create and manage Working Groups that focus on the most important areas of the network's establishment, stability, and growth. Some ideas for early Working Groups:

1. Librarians: Document the needs of libraries and the metadata fields and workflows that make the OME network valuable to OER libraries.
2. OME Nodes and Plugins: Document how libraries can use OME nodes to attach to the OME network to publish and subscribe to OER metadata records.
3. OME Network: Document how to manage, maintain, secure, and scale the OME network while ensuring that new libraries can rapidly join with minimal complexity.
4. Growth and Outreach: Manage the process of attracting new libraries, platforms, and contributors to the OME network.  Help other working groups understand the painpoints of new participants and help them to provide the documentation, tools and training to onboard more quickly and reliably.

These groups should not operate as isolated silos. They should be connected through a shared decision process that links governance, technical implementation, and community participation. Working groups should publish charter documents, meeting schedules, maintain meeting notes, and identify concrete deliverables, such as schema recommendations, onboarding guides, or deployment checklists. By creating pathways for contribution across library-oriented and technical roles, the project can attract a wide range of collaborators while keeping the community aligned with its open-access mission.

Sustainable governance also depends on a culture of openness, mentorship, and mutual accountability. The community should regularly invite new contributors into planning discussions, issue triage, documentation review, and usability testing. Good governance is not only a board structure; it is a system that makes it easy for volunteers to participate in meaningful ways, whether they are library professionals, system administrators, developers, or domain experts. The organization should be explicit about how decisions are made, how conflicts are resolved, and how relevant voices are included. That clarity will make OME more credible to institutions and more welcoming to contributors.

## Continuous Development

Continuous development should be organized around a disciplined and contributor-friendly process that supports both technical progress and community sustainability. The repository already reflects a strong architectural direction: a decentralized network built with Docker-based deployment, a Python application layer, plugin-based integrations, and a user-facing frontend connected to a shared metadata exchange. That structure provides a practical foundation for a healthy open source workflow. Contributors should work in clearly scoped areas such as metadata modeling, node operation, plugin development, onboarding tools, frontend usability, and documentation. Breaking work into smaller, reviewable units makes it easier for new contributors to join and for maintainers to keep the project moving forward without bottlenecks.

The community should establish a rolling 24-month development roadmap with quarterly milestones and measurable outputs. In the first 12 months, the priorities should include stabilizing the core network architecture, improving onboarding documentation, and demonstrating a small number of working reference integrations. In the second 12 months, the focus should shift to production readiness, stronger metadata quality practices, and wider interoperability across partner systems. The roadmap should be transparent, reviewed regularly, and published in a way that allows contributors to see how their work supports the broader mission of the network. The goal is not simply to produce code, but to build trust in the project's long-term viability as a shared infrastructure for OER exchange.

The project should also invest in the human systems that sustain open source communities. New contributors should be onboarded with clear guidance on how to participate in issues, reviews, testing, and release planning. Mentorship is particularly important in a project that spans library practice, open metadata standards, and technical implementation. Maintainers should create contributor guidelines, document technical standards, and encourage participation from librarians, catalogers, developers, and institutional stakeholders. A sustainable community grows when people can see meaningful ways to contribute beyond coding alone, including metadata curation, usability testing, documentation, partner outreach, and policy development.

The development process should reward quality and transparency rather than speed alone. The project can benefit from a release cadence that clearly communicates what changed, what was tested, and what remains in progress. Regular community check-ins, issue dashboards, and milestone tracking will help maintain momentum while making it easier to identify dependencies and gaps. At the same time, the community should be realistic about what can be achieved in a 24-month window. The central challenge is not to build a perfect network overnight, but to create a durable foundation: a governed organization, a repeatable technical stack, a small network of committed partners, and a contributor ecosystem that can help the project mature over time.

## Evaluation Plan

For the first 24 months, the evaluation plan should center on whether OME is becoming a reliable network infrastructure for partner institutions rather than on abstract measures such as raw page views or general “traffic.” The primary thrust of this period should be network establishment: creating a governance structure, building a strong frontend and user experience, and proving that a small set of partner sites can publish and subscribe to metadata records through the OME system. The project should focus on operational repeatability: if the infrastructure can be deployed consistently, onboard new partners, and support interoperable metadata exchange, then the network is moving toward real usefulness. This approach is more meaningful than generic engagement metrics because it measures whether the project functions as a durable piece of shared infrastructure.

The evaluation process should include measurable targets and benchmarks for the next 24 months. By the end of the first year, the project should have an active OER Steering Committee, at least three functioning working groups, and a repeatable deployment process for OME nodes, plugins, and frontend services across a pilot cohort of partner institutions. By the end of the second year, the project should demonstrate a small but meaningful network of libraries and platforms using OME to publish, exchange, or validate OER metadata. Success should be tracked using indicators such as:

- number of partner institutions participating in the network
- number of OME nodes and plugins deployed in pilot or production environments
- number of metadata records exchanged or curated across the network
- number of end-users, especially librarians, using the system in real workflows
- time required to onboard a new partner library
- number of governance and technical outputs produced by working groups, such as standards, procedures, and operational guides

These benchmarks should be reviewed regularly to assess whether the project is growing in a sustainable way. The plan should also consider innovation objectives. OME should move from being a read-only network or proof-of-concept environment to a read/write model that supports genuine contribution and community value. The most important innovation benchmark is not simply more data, but whether multiple libraries can integrate once and share metadata across many partners without creating redundant custom integrations. A small group of pilot partners should demonstrate this clearly: one integration, multiple participating sites, rapid onboarding, and repeated operational success. If these benchmarks are met, OME will have moved beyond a prototype and become a credible foundation for an open, collaborative OER network.

In addition to operational and technical benchmarks, the evaluation plan should assess community health. A healthy ecosystem will show a growing number of contributors, active working groups, clear governance decisions, and ongoing documentation stewardship. The project can track whether partner institutions feel supported by the community, whether onboarding reduces friction over time, and whether the network is producing sustainable value for libraries and other OER stakeholders. Over a 24-month horizon, the strongest measure of success will be that OME becomes easier to join, easier to operate, and easier to trust as a shared infrastructure for open educational resources.
